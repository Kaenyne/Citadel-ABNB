# How we estimated Airbnb's Q3 2026 from our own data, what it says, and what happens next

**Date:** 11 September 2026. **Author:** Krishang Surapaneni (compiled with Claude Code). **For:** the ABNB pitch team. **Source package:** PR #41 (`docs/q3nowcast/SYNTHESIS.md` and the four notes under `research/notes/q3nowcast/`).

## 1. The question and the answer in one paragraph

Airbnb's fiscal year is the calendar year, so Q3 2026 runs from 1 July to 30 September and gets reported on 5 November. On 11 September we are 73 days into the 92. We asked whether the data the team has collected can produce a defensible number for Q3 Nights and Seats Booked and for ADR before the print. The answer is yes for nights, with a wide band, and no for ADR from data alone. Our best read is **3Q26 nights growth of +9.5 to +10.0 percent year over year, band 8.5 to 11.0**, which is consistent with the team's existing baseline of +9.9 percent and does not support an acceleration from Q2's +10.3 percent. ADR is a structural estimate of **about +3.2 percent, band +1.6 to +4.6**, which implies revenue of roughly $4.80 billion against a guide of $4.69 to $4.77 billion. The beat itself is not the trade (Airbnb has beaten its guide 19 quarters out of 19). What matters is whether nights accelerate or decelerate and what management guides for Q4.

## 2. The datasets

We tested four kinds of data. Two are ours, two are external.

**Inside Airbnb reviews files.** Inside Airbnb scrapes Airbnb listings for about 120 cities and regions and publishes, for each, a `reviews.csv.gz` containing every review on every listing that is live at the time of the scrape, with the review date. We hold 363 of these files across 123 markets: dumps from June to August 2026 and, downloaded during this run, the matching dumps from August and September 2025. That is 8.9 GB of raw data in the shared store (`data/raw/inside_airbnb_reviews`, gitignored). A review is written after a stay, so the count of reviews by review date is a record of completed stays by month, going back years, for each market.

**Inside Airbnb calendar files.** For 34 markets we hold five calendar snapshots (September 2025 through August 2026), and this run found and downloaded 164 older snapshots from 2024 and 2025 that the CDN still serves, so the store now holds 328 files. A calendar file says, for each listing and each future date, whether it is available. Changes between snapshots are a booking-pace signal.

**External series.** We pulled and cached everything free that covers July to September 2026: weekly US hotel RevPAR from CoStar/STR press releases, TSA checkpoint counts, US international arrivals from the National Travel and Tourism Office, Spain's INE arrivals and hotel nights, Japan inbound, lodging CPI, and 96 quotes from 27 companies at September investor conferences. We also priced the paid sources we do not have (AirDNA, STR, card-spend panels, app data).

**The team's own disclosed-KPI history.** Airbnb's quarterly nights, GBV, revenue and ADR from the shareholder letters, plus the ADR decomposition already on main (FX effect, geographic mix, party size, length of stay, seats dilution).

## 3. Methodology for the nights number

The core idea is simple. If reviews are a stable fraction of stays, then the year-over-year change in review counts across many markets should track the year-over-year change in Airbnb's reported nights. Three problems have to be solved before that works, and each was solved rather than assumed.

**Problem one: survivorship.** A dump only contains reviews for listings that are still live. A listing that left the platform takes its whole review history with it. So in any single dump, older months are undercounted relative to recent months, and a naive year-over-year computed inside one dump is biased upward. We measured this by comparing the same review month across two dumps taken a year apart, on 129 market pairs. About 15 to 16 percent of a month's reviews disappear for each year of dump age. The effect on a within-dump year-over-year is about 20 percentage points of overstatement, and, critically, that wedge is stable over time (20.7 points in Q3 2026 to date, 20.4 in Q2 2026, 18.6 in Q3 2025). We therefore built two versions of the index: a within-dump version, which carries the wedge but has the longest history and is what the backtest uses, and a vintage-matched version, which compares the 2026 dump's July against the 2025 dump's July so both sides have the same survivorship, and which gives the clean level.

**Problem two: posting lag and partial months.** Reviews are posted zero to 14 days after check-out. We measured that completeness passes 98.5 percent at 14 days, so every window ends 14 days before the dump date, and the dump month itself is dropped as truncated. Each 2026 window is compared with the same window 364 days earlier so the day-of-week mix matches.

**Problem three: aggregation and composition.** Market-level growth rates are combined four ways: equal weights, review-count weights, the median, and Airbnb's FY2025 regional nights shares (North America 28.3 percent, EMEA 41.6, Latin America 17.9, Asia Pacific 12.3). A market-quarter enters the quarterly index only if all three months are present on both sides. A stable-listing variant, using only listings present in both years, was also built to see what same-store demand is doing.

**The backtest.** This is the part that decides whether the index is worth anything. We used the protocol the team set in the earlier alt-data note (workstream 08): fit a regression of Airbnb's disclosed nights growth on the index using only data before each quarter, predict that quarter, and score the error from Q1 2024 to Q2 2026 (ten quarters). The forecast is compared against three benchmarks that require no data at all: "same growth as last quarter" (naive), "same as a year ago", and a simple autoregression. A ratio below 1.0 means the index beats the benchmark. The global review-weighted index scored **0.68 against naive, 0.40 against prior year and 0.70 against the autoregression**. Dropping any one quarter keeps the naive ratio between 0.63 and 0.86, so no single quarter carries the result. We ran 2,560 variants; the family sits at 0.65 to 0.75, and we report the family, not the best cell. First differences never beat naive, and the one-quarter-lagged version is only marginal, so this is a coincident indicator, not a leading one. As a second reality check, the European markets' review counts were tested monthly against Eurostat's platform-nights series and beat naive there too (0.59).

**From partial quarter to Q3.** The July and August windows cover about 60 to 65 percent of the quarter. We measured how much the quarter-to-date growth differed from full-quarter growth in 2023, 2024 and 2025 inside the same dump (0.1 to 0.6 points, standard deviation 0.2) and applied that gap. Then the full-quarter index is mapped to nights through the fitted regression (slope 0.32 nights-points per index-point, fitted on 14 quarters). The band combines the out-of-sample forecast error with the gap uncertainty.

## 4. The results

**Quarter to date, survivorship-clean.** Stays in the 123-market panel grew +3.8 percent in July and +8.2 percent in the first half of August, for +5.2 percent Q3 to date on review weights and +6.0 percent on nights-share weights. Q2 on the same construction was +4.3 and +5.6. By region: North America +6.9, EMEA +0.9, Latin America +27.5, Asia Pacific +7.4. The median market is +2.2 percent and 77 of 119 markets are positive.

**Mapped to Airbnb nights.** The headline within-dump row gives +10.0 percent with a band of 8.6 to 11.5. The other six weighting and construction rows give 8.6 to 10.0, and anchoring on the Q2 print instead of the regression intercept gives 9.8 to 10.8. Taken together: **+9.5 percent, band 8.5 to 11.0**. Naive "same as Q2" would say 10.3, a year ago was 8.8, and the team baseline from the H1-to-H2 bridge and PR #32 is 9.9. All three sit inside the band.

**What the panel cannot see.** Airbnb's total nights grow faster than our panel's stays (+9 to 10 versus +4 to 7) because Airbnb's growth is in expansion markets and in listings under a year old that a panel of 123 regulated cities under-represents. That is why the mapping needs a fitted intercept and why the index reads direction and rough size, not the level. The stable-listing variant is actually negative (-3.3 percent in Q2, -7.4 in July): all of the platform's growth in these mature markets is coming from new supply. The index also counts stays, not bookings. Airbnb's KPI counts bookings made in the quarter, including bookings for Q4 and Q1 stays, so a late-September booking shift, which is where Reserve Now Pay Later and hurricanes act, is invisible to any stay-based series. Reviews per stay is unobserved and could drift; one point of review-propensity change looks identical to one point of demand.

**Corroboration from outside.** Weekly US hotel RevPAR ran +8.2 percent in July and faded to about +4 percent by mid-August, with hotel ADR growth collapsing from +5.7 to +0.6 percent. Hotel RevPAR now beats naive on Airbnb nights with 2026 added (0.665), and US inbound arrivals turned out to be a new survivor (0.72) that nobody in the repo had used. Stacking the twelve external series that are knowable today gives +9.2 percent with a wide range of 6.8 to 12.0. Marriott said July RevPAR was +7 percent, +5 excluding the World Cup, and Expedia said July was consistent with Q2. Airbnb management gave no quarter-to-date number at any September venue. The calendar booking-pace metric, by contrast, failed its backtest (the sign was inverted on five quarters) and is kept only as a descriptive cross-check; it reads flat to slightly up.

**ADR.** No free source measures realised ADR, so the ADR number is built from parts: the FX effect (between -1.1 and +0.3 points depending on estimator), geographic mix (-1.2 points), party-size, length-of-stay and new-business dilution terms, and the like-for-like pricing residual that carried the first half. The component build fails its own walk-forward test (it is biased low into accelerations), so the naive "same as H1" route (+4.0 ex-FX) is carried alongside it. The card lands at +3.2 percent reported, $176.8, band $174.0 to $179.1. Management's own words on 6 August were "a moderate increase in ADR due to mix shift and price appreciation." Hotel ADR and lodging CPI both softened through August, so ADR is the softer line of the two.

## 5. What this means for the pitch

First, the deceleration base case now has alternative-data support rather than only a seasonal pattern. Every independent read lands at or slightly below the Q2 rate. Nothing points to the +10.5 percent or higher that our pre-registered card defines as the surprise. Second, the regional texture is consistent across sources: Latin America very strong, North America mid-to-high single digits and improving into August, EMEA close to flat on a stay basis, Asia Pacific mid-to-high single digits. The soft EMEA summer is the most useful new fact. Third, the index is the one piece of team-built alternative data that beats a naive forecast out of sample, is reproducible from public files, and corrects a bias (survivorship) that anyone using this data casually would miss. That combination is what a judge will credit, so it should be the alt-data exhibit, shown with the survivorship wedge and the coverage table.

## 6. Next steps: what, why, when

1. **Pull the missing August 2026 batch now.** Inside Airbnb is serving an unlisted August wave for 86 markets and our disk holds about 49 of them; Los Angeles, San Francisco, Chicago, Boston, London, Paris, Barcelona, Singapore, Sydney and Toronto are missing. Adding them raises the share of the index with August data above the current 88 percent and, more importantly, fills the North America and EMEA rows where the big absentees sit. This is running as branch `krish/aug-batch-refresh`; the pipeline is checkpointed per file, so a rerun takes about ten minutes once files are down.
2. **Re-run when the September dumps land, roughly 10 to 30 September.** That closes the August half-month and adds the first September days, bringing the quarter to about 80 to 85 percent observed before the 2 October pitch. Commands: E1 discover, E2 download, then E3 through E7. Same for the calendar probe (F0b).
3. **Name one ADR-FX estimator before the model is frozen.** The euro-only fit backtests better (0.46 versus 0.54 points of error); the basket build says Q4 ADR gets 1.7 points more FX help. This single choice moves the Q4 ADR line more than anything else we measured.
4. **Decide on the Q4 lap question from the overnight run.** The RNPL ledger dated the cancellation-policy redesign and single fee as global from October 2025. If they lap outside North America as well, the Q4 nights baseline moves from 8.9 to 8.0 to 8.2 percent. This is arithmetic on sourced dates plus one pinned split and needs a team decision, not more data.
5. **Score the print on 5 November.** Add Q3 to the walk-forward and see whether the 0.68 ratio survives an eleventh quarter. If the print falls inside 8.6 to 11.5 the index did its job. Also score ADR ex-FX against +3.5 percent and note whether management restates the product-bundle contribution figure it gave in Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 but dropped in Q2.
6. **Do not** widen the panel with agent-scraped listings (the quote index test showed scraped data reproduces the dump's composition) and do not use the calendar-flow metric as a nights input.

## 8. Update, later on 11 September: the August 2026 batch pull

The external-sources workstream had reported that Inside Airbnb was serving an unlisted August 2026 batch for 86 markets and that we held only about 49 of them. A full probe of the CDN (5,655 requests, no errors) showed that premise was stale: all 123 markets have an August 2026 reviews dump and 122 were already in our store, including Los Angeles, San Francisco, Chicago, Boston, London, Paris, Barcelona, Singapore, Sydney and Toronto. The one true gap was Tokyo, and the cause was a bug, not a missing file: the discover script probed the path `japan/kanto/tokyo` while the CDN spells it with a macron, so every Tokyo request returned a 403. That is fixed, and Tokyo's August 2026 and August 2025 dumps are now in the store. No September 2026 dump exists yet for any market, and the 34 calendar markets already held their August calendar and listings files.

Did it improve the data? Slightly, and only in Asia Pacific. Markets with both July and August data went from 111 to 112, or from 88.4 to 90.4 percent of the 3Q25 review base, and APAC's own coverage rose from 51 to 68 percent of its base. North America and EMEA did not change: New York, Los Angeles, San Francisco and Montreal still drop out of the August row because their dumps are dated 10 August, which after the 14-day trim ends before August begins. The quarter-to-date global read moved from +5.2 to +5.5 percent, the implied nights headline stayed at +9.5 percent with the 8.5 to 11.0 band, and the backtest ratio moved from 0.683 to 0.681. The Q3 read stands.

One thing worth telling the team from the market-level table: the big US cities are strong on a vintage-matched basis (Boston +22.9 percent, New York +22.8, Chicago +12.5, Los Angeles +12.4, San Francisco +7.0) while the European capitals are flat to negative (London +9.7 but on a scope-change screen, Barcelona +1.1, Paris -3.0). That matches the soft-EMEA-summer reading in section 5. It is descriptive, from a survivorship-corrected panel, not a forecast by itself.
